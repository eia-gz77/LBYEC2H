\subsection{Signal Modeling}

The architectural block diagram illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:model} details the complete signal modeling and recovery utilized in this study. The forward modeling process initiates with the synthesis of the target astrophysical event, defined as a linear frequency-modulated (LFM) chirp spanning the 30--300~Hz band, denoted as the input $x(t)$. To replicate the physical interference actually observed at the detector, this ideal signal undergoes convolution with empirical LIGO strain data. This strain data serves as the system's impulse response, $h(t)$. The resulting operation yields the distorted output $y(t)$, representing the contaminated data stream that an interferometer would theoretically record.

The subsequent phase of the model attempts to mathematically invert this environmental degradation. Both the distorted output $y(t)$ and the known system impulse response $h(t)$ are converted into the frequency domain via a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), producing $Y(f)$ and $H(f)$, respectively. Spectral deconvolution is then executed through a direct division operation: $Y(f) / (H(f) + \epsilon)$. The inclusion of the regularization parameter, $\epsilon$, appears strictly necessary in this framework. Without it, attempting division in spectral regions where the detector sensitivity approaches zero would likely cause asymptotic noise amplification, destabilizing the entire numerical array. Following this stabilized inversion, an Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) returns the intermediate estimated signal, $\hat{X}(f)$, back to the time domain.

While spectral division retrieves the primary geometry of the chirp, it inevitably introduces high-frequency computational artifacts alongside residual 60~Hz harmonics. Relying solely on discrete deconvolution may prove insufficient for precise waveform reconstruction, suggesting that further physical conditioning is required. Therefore, the final stage routes the intermediate recovered signal into four parallel continuous-time filter networks. The signal is convolved independently with Butterworth, Chebyshev I, Chebyshev II, and Elliptic transfer functions, denoted as $H(s)$. Each operation yields a distinct output (Result 1 through 4), facilitating a direct comparative analysis of how varying magnitude responses and phase characteristics affect the final attenuation of out-of-band interference.

\subsection{Fourier Transform and Deconvolution Analysis}

The recovery process begins by addressing the convolution that occurred in the physical sensing environment, where the original chirp signal was convolved with the system's impulse response to produce the distorted output. To reverse this effect, the analysis shifts from the time domain to the frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Once in the frequency domain, deconvolution is performed by dividing the FFT of the distorted signal by the FFT of the system’s known impulse response. To ensure numerical stability and prevent mathematical errors during this division, a regularization parameter ($\epsilon = 10^{-6}$) is incorporated into the denominator \cite{Oppenheim2010}. After the signal’s original characteristics are isolated through this division, the Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) is applied to return the signal to the time domain. This sequence successfully extracts the raw chirp from the interference, though it often leaves behind high-frequency artifacts that require subsequent analog-informed filtering to achieve a clean reconstruction \cite{Proakis2007}.

\subsection{Analog Filter Design}

Analog filter design is an important step to isolate the recovered chirp from the high-frequency artifacts generated during deconvolution. Initially, a Butterworth bandpass filter was modeled for its characteristic maximally flat magnitude response in the passband, ensuring that the 30 Hz to 300 Hz chirp frequencies are preserved without amplitude distortion \cite{Proakis2007}. To enhance the transition from the passband to the stopband, Chebyshev Type I and Type II filters were also used. The Type I configuration provides a steeper roll-off by allowing for equiripple behavior in the passband, while the Type II filter maintains a flat passband by placing ripples in the stopband to improve specific frequency attenuation \cite{Oppenheim2010}. Furthermore, the Elliptic filter was implemented to achieve the sharpest possible roll-off between the passband and stopband by distributing ripples across both regions, which is essential for isolating low-frequency signals. These analog specifications were then mapped into the digital domain using the Bilinear Transformation, which uses the $s$-to-$z$ plane mapping to maintain the structural integrity of the analog transfer functions within a discrete-time simulation \cite{Proakis2007, Oppenheim2010}. This ensures that the high-frequency artifacts and noise in the initial deconvolution process are effectively suppressed \cite{Proakis2007}.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

The transition from theoretical signal models to a functional simulation requires a precise mapping of continuous-time analog concepts into the discrete-time digital domain. This process begins with the mathematical derivation of the system's difference equations, utilizing the Bilinear Transformation to convert the analog transfer functions ($s$-domain) of the Butterworth, Chebyshev, and Elliptic filters into digital filters ($z$-domain) while preserving their frequency response characteristics \cite{Proakis2007}. The implementation processes the raw, deconvolved data through these digital representations of analog circuitry to isolate the 30 Hz to 300 Hz sweep from the environmental 60 Hz power-line interference. To verify the effectiveness of the design, the recovered waveforms are analyzed through both time-domain comparison and spectrogram analysis, which provides a visual "chirp" trajectory to confirm the successful mitigation of high-frequency artifacts \cite{Oppenheim2010}. This dual-domain verification demonstrates that the digital implementation of these specific analog filter topologies provides a robust mechanism for extracting clean gravitational wave profiles from complex, noise-heavy datasets \cite{Proakis2007}.
